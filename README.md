# paybands

**This project's "80% confident" salary band really does cover 80% of salaries — 82.3%
measured across 10 splits (79.4% – 87.3%), against a nominal 80%. It is also 2.40x as wide as
its own midpoint. A typical answer is ₹4,91,660 – ₹39,84,224 around a midpoint of ₹13,95,079,
and nobody can make an offer from that.**

That is the headline result, and it is a negative one, so it goes first. The band is honest
and unusable at the same time, and those two facts have the same cause. Seven of the
strongest known drivers of an Indian tech salary — location tier, product-versus-services,
institute tier, internal level, previous CTC, performance rating, offer date — are simply
not in the public survey this is built on. Two candidates identical on everything the survey
*does* record can legitimately be 2-3x apart on the things it does not. A band that is
honest about that has to be wide.

The model is not broken. It has almost nothing to condition on, and it says so. Narrowing
the band without adding information would not make it more useful — it would just move it
back to the raw quantile band, which promises 80% and delivers **74.9%**. That version is
narrower (1.94x) and lies. Honesty cost **23.4% more width**, which is the correct trade and
the reason this README leads with the width rather than the coverage.

The interesting work here is the measurement, not the model: checking whether an interval's
confidence promise is true, validating a bias audit against a gap we injected on purpose,
and showing — with a negative control — that deleting the gender column removes about three
fifths of a pay gap and leaves the rest.

Everything below is regenerated by one command, `uv run python scripts/run_analysis.py`, and
written up with its caveats in [`docs/findings.md`](docs/findings.md).

If you want the whole story rather than the result — how the project was chosen, every decision,
every mistake and what it cost — [`docs/JOURNEY.md`](docs/JOURNEY.md) is that, written for someone
who has never done machine learning before.

## The numbers

Every comparison runs across **10 independent train/test splits** (seeds 0-9) and is reported
as a mean with the full range. On ~204 test rows a single split moves the headline MAE by
more than 10%, so a single-split number is an anecdote.

| Claim | Result |
|---|---|
| Group-median lookup beats "everyone earns the median" | **19.4% MAE** (13.1% – 26.0%), on 10/10 splits |
| Band model beats the lookup table | **11.9% MAE** (7.4% – 17.5%), on 10/10 splits |
| ...and still beats it when the lookup gets the extra 20% of data it doesn't need to calibrate | 11.0% (6.8% – 15.7%), 10/10 |
| Raw quantile band, promised 80% | **74.9% coverage** (70.1% – 84.3%) |
| After conformal calibration | **82.3% coverage** (79.4% – 87.3%) |
| Band width ÷ midpoint, raw → calibrated | 1.94x → **2.40x** (2.05 – 3.44) |
| Fairness audit, injected gaps of 0/3/8/15/25% | recovered **-0.34 / 2.67 / 7.69 / 14.71 / 24.75%** — worst error 0.34 pts, truth inside the CI 5/5 |
| Gap surviving deletion of the gender column | **38.4%** (34.3% – 41.0%), at an accuracy cost of 1.3% |
| Payroll calculator against a real payslip PDF | gross ₹50,000 (basic 26,000 + HRA 13,000 + special 11,000) → PF ₹1,800, medical ₹300, **net ₹47,900**, CTC ₹51,800. Every line exact. |

The lookup table in row one is four lines of pandas. It is the number the model had to beat,
and it is genuinely hard to beat — the win is **11%**, not the 3x a bare R² would let you
imply. Building it first is the reason that sentence can be said with a number attached.

## Three things this project does that a salary notebook usually doesn't

### 1. The uncertainty was measured, not assumed

Asking LightGBM for the 90th percentile is not the same as getting it. Between the pinball
loss and the answer sit a finite training set, a 7-leaf model, and a test set nobody has
seen, and the promise leaks out through all three. Nothing in the output announces this. You
only find out by counting.

Counted at five confidence levels rather than one — one level checked is a spot check, five
is a calibration curve:

| promised | raw delivered | conformal delivered |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | 43.0% | 48.5% |
| 60% | 53.1% | 59.8% |
| 70% | 63.1% | 67.3% |
| 80% | 72.9% | 81.7% |
| 90% | 86.8% | 90.4% |

The raw band under-covers at **5 of 5** levels, by a suspiciously constant ~7 points. The
calibrated band's worst miss is **-2.7 points**. The fix is conformalised quantile regression
(Romano, Patterson & Candès, 2019): one number `q̂` computed on a calibration set the model
has never seen — here 0.1573 log points on average (0.0469 – 0.2508) — added to both edges.
Fifteen lines to measure, twenty more to fix. See `reports/calibration_raw.png` and
`reports/calibration_conformal.png`.

Training three separate quantile models rather than putting one error bar around one
prediction also paid off: band width in rupees grows **5.0x** from the bottom decile of
predictions to the top. The band is learned, not a fixed percentage.

### 2. The fairness audit is calibrated against a truth we wrote

You cannot validate a bias detector on real data, because nobody knows the real gap. So the
audit is pointed at synthetic data generated by rules we chose, with a pay gap we injected,
and asked to give it back.

| injected | recovered | 95% CI | truth inside CI? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0%** | **-0.34%** | -1.39% to +0.71% | yes |
| 3% | 2.67% | 1.65% – 3.68% | yes |
| 8% | 7.69% | 6.72% – 8.65% | yes |
| 15% | 14.71% | 13.81% – 15.60% | yes |
| 25% | 24.75% | 23.95% – 25.53% | yes |

The row that matters most is the first. At an injected gap of zero the audit reports
**-0.34%** — it does not invent bias in fair data. An audit that cries wolf is worse than no
audit, because it is confidently wrong in the direction that gets someone accused.

The same run with the proxy channel left out of the controls gets every estimate too high by
2.2 to 2.9 points, and **0 of 5** intervals contain the truth. Neither table is wrong; they
answer different questions, and both are published rather than whichever is more comfortable.

### 3. Deleting the protected column does not delete the bias

"We removed gender from the training data, so the model can't be biased" is a widespread
claim. This is a controlled refutation of it, with a negative control.

The same model, fitted twice on synthetic data carrying an 8% injected gap — once with the
gender column, once without — and the gap measured on held-out predictions:

| seed | gap with gender | gap without | surviving | accuracy cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 10.5% | 3.6% | 34.3% | +2.3% |
| 1 | 9.4% | 3.8% | 41.0% | +0.7% |
| 2 | 11.4% | 4.5% | 39.8% | +0.8% |

**Mean 38.4% of the gap survives deletion, at a mean accuracy cost of 1.3%.** The remainder
travels through `career_gap_months` — a feature that predicts pay honestly *and* carries
group membership (association 0.367 against gender, versus 0.004 or less for almost every
other column). Nothing was even traded away for the harm that remains.

That correlation scan runs before any model is trained, which is why it should be the first
thing pointed at company data. Our synthetic world has exactly one proxy channel because we
built it with one. A real dataset has several and nobody labels them.

## Three layers, and one rule

```
LAYER 1 — MODEL       candidate → base salary band          learned     changes when the market moves
LAYER 2 — CALCULATOR  base salary → PF, insurance, tax      arithmetic  changes when the Budget does
LAYER 3 — POLICY      band + rating + budget → increment    rules       changes when HR decides
```

The rule behind the split: **never make a model learn something you can compute.** PF is a
percentage, insurance is a flat premium, income tax is a published slab table. A model asked
to learn those would be less accurate than a formula, would break every February, and — the
real damage — would confuse *"the market moved"* with *"tax slabs changed"*. Train on
take-home, watch take-home fall when the Budget raises rates, and the model concludes
engineers are worth less. Every prediction after that is poisoned.

**Layer 3 contains no machine learning on purpose.** An increment is a decision, not a
pattern: how big the pool is, what a rating of 4 is worth in rupees, whether to correct
someone's underpayment this year or over three. A model trained on last year's raises would
repeat them, including the wrong ones, and would repeat them *unarguably*. The rules layer
says "8% because your rating was 3, plus 5% because you sit at 0.86 of band". Only one of
those can be challenged by the person it is about, and being challengeable is the job.

The number tying the layers together is the **compa-ratio** — actual salary ÷ predicted
midpoint. Below 0.90 is the underpaid list; the increment that brings someone to band is the
recommendation. One division, both use cases, no second model.

Layer 2 is validated the only way a calculator should be: against an actual payslip PDF. Gross
₹50,000 (basic ₹26,000 + HRA ₹13,000 + special ₹11,000) → PF ₹1,800, medical ₹300, net ₹47,900,
CTC ₹51,800. Every line matches, and the payslip's own annual tax projection (gross ₹6,00,000 →
taxable ₹5,25,000) reproduces too, which checks the tax path separately from the deduction path.

**The figures throughout this repository are anonymised.** They are the shape of a real payslip —
same component split, same binding PF ceiling, same CTC-versus-gross gap, same nil tax under the
87A rebate — with the amounts changed. The validation was done against genuine documents; the
documents are not in the repository.

That validation replaced an earlier one built on figures reported from memory, which had the
basic fraction, the PF ceiling rule and the insurance premium all wrong — and passed, because
the config had been tuned until it did. **A test and a config derived from the same mistaken
belief agree with each other perfectly and tell you nothing.** Only a document settled it.

Every rate lives in `configs/payroll/fy_2025_26.yaml`, one file per financial year, never edited
once written, so a past year's take-home can always be recomputed.

Full reasoning in [`docs/design.md`](docs/design.md).

## What I got wrong, and what surprised me

**Education has negative importance. Shuffling it improves the model.** Permutation
importance on held-out rows: **-₹2,675**, or -0.20% of MAE. `skill_java`, `experience_bucket`
and `skill_rust` do the same. `docs/design.md` predicted education would be "weaker than
people expect once experience is accounted for". It is weaker than that — on 613 training
rows the model is leaning on noise in the column, and taking the noise away helps. Education
is now on the list of fields to *drop* from the company data request.

**`n_skills` ranks second on split-count importance and is worth 0.25% of the error.** It is
an integer running 0-20, so a tree can split on it at many thresholds, and it climbs to
**12.3%** of split count — ahead of `org_size`. Shuffle it and the model loses **₹3,318**,
against **+23.13% of MAE** for `years_experience`. Split count measures how many questions a
feature *could* answer, not how useful the answers were. Anyone reporting LightGBM's default
importance is reporting cardinality as much as signal. This is the live demonstration, which
is why the script computes all three measures and prints the disagreement.

**The error is concentrated, not spread.** Mean absolute bias in roles with fewer than 5 test
rows per split is **40.8%**; in the rest it is **10.3%**. The worst cell is QA — 55.6%
coverage against an 80% promise and a -64.2% bias, on 8 survey respondents. Security is the
mirror image at +56.1%, on 5. Meanwhile Fullstack, Backend and Frontend sit at -2.8%, +2.0%
and +0.2%. This is not a mediocre model; it is a decent model next to a catastrophic one, and
the catastrophic half is exactly the roles a company would most want a band for, because
they are the ones nobody in the room has an intuition about. Concentrated error is fixable by
collecting 200 QA rows. Average error is not.

**The survey has no protected-attribute column at all, so the fairness audit — the part of
the project that works best — cannot be run on it.** 172 columns were checked for `gender`,
`ethnic`, `race`, `sexuality`, `orientation`, `transgender`, `disability`, `accessibility`,
`nationality`, `religion`, `caste`. Every one returns zero. This is not missing values; the
question is not in the survey, and no imputation or join recovers it. The only demographic
field is `Age`, and on the 702 people it covers the adjusted gap is -5.22% with a 95%
interval running from -33.6% to +17.1%. The honest reading of a 51-point interval is "we
cannot tell", which is a different sentence from "no gap".

**Three headline numbers were first reported from too few seeds and had to be corrected.**
The baseline improvement, the model's margin over the lookup, and the calibrated coverage all
moved once the comparison went to 10 splits with the range printed alongside the mean. That
is why `spread()` in the analysis script physically cannot print a mean without its range —
the guardrail exists because I needed it.

**Unresolved:** the raw band's coverage shortfall is -7.0, -6.9, -6.9, -7.1 points at four of
five levels. Four numbers within 0.2 points of each other is not noise. Best guess is pinball
loss on 613 rows with `min_child_samples=25` pulling the extreme quantiles inward, testable
by sweeping that parameter. We fixed the symptom with conformal calibration without
diagnosing the cause — fine engineering, unfinished science.

## What the data is, and what it cannot support

Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025, public CSV: 49,191 responses, 172 columns.

```
survey responses            49,191
from India                   2,547
reporting salary in INR      2,463
  − no salary reported        1,235  (50.1%)
  − below ₹100,000              200  (8.1%)
  − above ₹20,000,000             6  (0.2%)
usable rows                  1,022
```

**Half the Indian respondents who reached the currency question declined to state a salary.**
That is selection bias, not missing data, and no cleaning rule touches it. "I'd rather not
say" is very unlikely to be independent of the number. This is the largest single caveat on
the project.

**Self-reported and unaudited.** People round up, some report CTC where others report base,
and one person typed 23 nines — an entry of 1.0e+23 rupees that dragged the mean of the raw
column to ₹8.14 × 10^19 and moved the median not at all. One typo, eighteen orders of
magnitude, and that is the entire argument for why every baseline here predicts a median.

**Stack Overflow respondents are not a random sample of Indian developers.** They skew
English-speaking, internationally oriented and senior; median experience in the surviving
sample is 6 years. **Salaries here are probably overstated relative to the actual Indian
market**, and a band built from them will quote high. We cannot measure by how much.

**Three roles are 72.5% of the sample.** Six roles have fewer than 20 respondents each.

Skew is +2.86 in rupees and -0.23 in log rupees, which is the evidence behind modelling
log(salary) rather than an assertion about it (`reports/dist_raw_vs_log.png`).

The list of seven absent pay drivers, and what each would be expected to change, is in
[`docs/findings.md`](docs/findings.md) §1 and §6. The short version: this is not a weak model
of Indian salaries, it is an honest model of the four or five things a public survey happens
to ask about, and the fix is different data rather than better hyperparameters.

## Reproducing every number

```bash
brew install libomp                                        # macOS only, see below
uv sync --extra dev --extra data --extra model --extra api --extra explain
uv run python scripts/fetch_data.py                        # 134MB survey CSV
uv run pytest                                              # 441 tests
uv run python scripts/run_analysis.py                      # ~30s, prints everything
```

The survey CSV is not committed — 134MB, not ours, and reproducible from a URL. `fetch_data.py`
downloads it to `data/public/` and is idempotent, so re-running it costs nothing. It pins the
2025 file rather than a "latest" alias deliberately: if the data changed underneath us, every
number below would quietly stop being reproducible.

`scripts/run_analysis.py` is one file that runs top to bottom, seeds every random draw from a
local `Generator` rather than numpy's global state, prints every number in
`docs/findings.md`, and writes every chart to `reports/`. **No number appears in the findings
document that the script does not print.** Charts in `reports/` are gitignored — they are
derived artefacts, rebuilt from code, not files to be kept in sync by hand.

A notebook would record the order I happened to click in. Six weeks later, "which settings
produced this number?" needs an answer.

**Why `libomp`:** LightGBM links against OpenMP, which Apple's toolchain doesn't ship. Without
it you get `Library not loaded: @rpath/libomp.dylib` at import time — a linker error, not a
Python one, so `pip install` looks like it succeeded. Linux and Windows don't need it.

**Why `shap` sits in its own extra:** its transitive `llvmlite`/`numba` pins conflict with
Python 3.12 in some resolutions and would block the whole install. It is only needed for
explanations, so it lives in `--extra explain`. Don't fold it back into `model`.

## The service

```
POST /predict-band   candidate → band, computed take-home, confidence flag, caveat, explanation
POST /compa-ratio    candidate + actual salary → ratio and the word HR would use for it
GET  /health         is a model loaded, which one, which payroll config year
```

One rule is enforced in `service.py` and typed in `models.py`: **no salary number leaves the
service without the width of the band it came from and a plain-English caveat.** A band wider
than 50% of its midpoint comes back with `decision_grade: false` and a caveat saying so — at
2.40x, that is most of them.

A wide band returns 200, not an error. Refusing to answer would be worse: the caller works
around it, and a workaround has no caveat attached. The honest design is to answer *and* say
what the answer is worth.

Per-prediction SHAP explanations live in `src/paybands/explain/` and are served with the
band, because a recruiter must be able to argue with the number. A salary figure with no
reasoning attached cannot be challenged, and an unchallengeable salary figure is exactly the
thing that causes harm.

## Layout

```
src/paybands/
  data/        loaders — public, synthetic and company data, all one table shape
  features/    experience encodings, skill flags, location tiers
  model/       baselines, quantile band model, conformal calibration, splits, metrics
  fairness/    the gap audit and the proxy scan
  explain/     per-prediction SHAP
  payroll/     Layer 2 — the deduction calculator, config-driven
  policy/      Layer 3 — increment and pay-equity rules, no ML
  api/         FastAPI service
  plots/       every chart in reports/
configs/       payroll rates and policy rules, versioned by financial year
scripts/       run_analysis.py — regenerates every number and chart
tests/         441 tests
docs/          design.md (architecture), findings.md (the evidence)
```

All three loaders emit the same table shape, so company data drops in and everything
downstream works — already tested, before the real data exists. `data/company/` is gitignored,
and the rule was written before the folder had anything in it.

`src/paybands/features/location.py` maps cities to tiers and contributes nothing today,
because the survey has no city column. It is written and tested now so that the day real
salary data arrives is not also the day I first write the code that consumes it.

## What would change with company data

In order of what matters. Full table with evidence in [`docs/findings.md`](docs/findings.md) §6.

1. **The fairness audit becomes runnable at all.** Binary, not incremental. Today the answer
   is "unanswerable"; with a protected attribute it is a number with a confidence interval.
2. **Location tier and product-versus-services enter the model.** Both absent, both named in
   `design.md` as among the largest gaps in Indian pay. A chunk of what the model currently
   calls irreducible uncertainty is Bangalore-versus-Indore.
3. **Thin roles get populated.** 40.8% bias versus 10.3% is a sample-size problem with a
   known fix.
4. **Non-response and self-reporting disappear.** Payroll has neither.
5. **A temporal split becomes mandatory**, and the honest score will be *worse* than the
   numbers above. That lower number will be the true one.
6. **The previous-salary experiment becomes possible** — train with and without last-drawn
   CTC and compare accuracy against fairness. Anchoring to last salary is standard practice in
   India and the most effective way to make one early underpayment follow someone for a
   career. It is currently untested in either direction.

Whether the band becomes decision-grade is the open bet. It is 2.40x wide because the model
conditions on four or five columns. Add five strong ones and it should narrow substantially —
but by how much is unknown, and it is possible that Indian salary variance at fixed
observables is simply large. That would itself be worth knowing.
